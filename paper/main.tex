\begin{abstract}
Folklore holds that costly behaviors are best spread by seeding dense
clusters; the wide-bridges literature supplies the rationale. We revisit this
on synthetic two-layer organizations---a formal hierarchy and an informal
network of dense small teams---with fractional-threshold dynamics:
heterogeneous thresholds, a zero-threshold innovator atom,
credibility-weighted exposure, ready/willing/able gating. Under equal
budgets: (i)~broadcast converts only innovators (exactly zero adoption
without them), and sustained campaigning does not repair the threshold
arithmetic; (ii)~contrary to folklore, \emph{scattered} seeding robustly beats
cluster seeding on reach---every scattered seed is already embedded in a dense
team---and this survives removing the innovator atom; (iii)~the ordering
\emph{reverses} under reinforcement-dependent decay: scattered gains evaporate
while cluster-seeded adoption persists---a reach--retention trade-off, not a
dominant strategy; (iv)~global visibility of adoption is exactly threshold
inflation ($\theta/v$): invisibility starves all strategies uniformly, while
seed-targeted visibility helps seeds in proportion to their outward
credibility; (v)~knockouts find no load-bearing individuals. All data are
synthetic; results are bit-for-bit reproducible from one master seed.
\keywords{complex contagion, threshold models, seeding strategies,
organizational networks, agent-based simulation}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and related work}

Threshold models~\cite{granovetter1978} formalize why costly behaviors spread
differently from information: adoption requires reinforcement from multiple
credible sources. Cascades on random graphs obey a vulnerability
boundary~\cite{watts2002}; \emph{complex contagions} need wide bridges of
overlapping ties, which long-range shortcuts destroy~\cite{centola2007,centola2010}.
Network-intervention practice~\cite{valente2012} distilled this into a popular
prescription: \emph{seed dense clusters, never scatter}---evidenced largely in
populations not otherwise embedded in cohesive groups. Real organizations are
\emph{modular}: nearly everyone already sits inside a dense small team. What
does cluster seeding buy when every possible seed is already surrounded by a
cluster? We study this with a transparent mechanistic agent-based model (not
LLM personas, whose validity is contested~\cite{argyle2023,bisbee2024,wu2025}),
with every modeling decision logged publicly (Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

A generator produces two-layer organizations: a formal hierarchy (CEO, 8
departments, $\sim$250 teams of mean size 8) and an informal influence
network---dense within teams ($p{=}0.9$), locally concentrated between
ring-adjacent ``sister'' teams, sparse across departments (silo strength
$s{=}0.85$), plus tenure-homophilous noise. Adoption propagates only on the
informal layer. Agent $i$ holds threshold
$\theta_i \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\mu\kappa,(1-\mu)\kappa)$, $\mu{=}0.30$, and with
probability $p_{\mathrm{innov}}{=}0.025$ is an \emph{innovator}
($\theta_i{=}0$). Exposure is the credibility-weighted share of contacts whose
adoption is visible,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{share}_i(t) = \frac{\sum_{j\in N(i)} v_j\, w_{ij}\, a_j(t{-}1) + b(t)}
                           {\sum_{j\in N(i)} w_{ij} + b(t)},
\label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
with credibility $w$ (close collaborator $1.0>$ manager $0.7>$ distant peer
$0.6>$ central comms $0.3$), source visibility $v_j\in[0,1]$ (default 1), and
$b(t)$ a transient comms term active only while a broadcast runs. Adoption
requires \emph{ready} ($\mathrm{share}_i\ge\theta_i$ and $\mathrm{share}_i>0$,
i.e.\ awareness), \emph{willing} (Bernoulli $0.85$), and \emph{able} ($1.0$
here); updates are synchronous. Optional decay: adopters relapse with
probability $\rho$ per step while $\mathrm{share}_i<\theta_i$. Five
equal-budget strategies ($5\%$ of $N{=}2000$): \emph{broadcast} (zero
seeds---one step of universal awareness via comms), \emph{random},
\emph{champions} (top informal degree), \emph{cluster} (whole teams),
\emph{line-managers-first}. Each condition: 50 replicates (sweeps: 12), fresh
organization each, all from one master seed.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{Reach (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}).} At $\kappa{=}20$, broadcast converts
essentially only the innovators, stalling at $5.5\pm3.4\%$ (range
1.8--14.5\%); cluster seeding builds local critical mass and reaches
$31.9\pm9.2\%$ before silos cap it---the distributions do not overlap across
$2\times50$ replicates (broadcast max $14.5\%<$ cluster min $17.1\%$). But the
folklore part fails: \emph{scattered} strategies dominate reach (random
$69.5\pm11.1\%$, champions $73.4\pm7.0\%$). In a population of dense small
teams every scattered seed is already a latent cluster seed; one hundred
team-ignition lotteries beat twelve guaranteed saturations. Under broader heterogeneity ($\kappa{=}12$) broadcast becomes a lottery
($19.8\pm12.5\%$, range 4.9--64.5\%); the ordering is unchanged. Which regime
a real organization inhabits is open: thresholds are unobserved.

\textbf{Innovators carry broadcast---not the scattering advantage
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pinnov}).} At $p_{\mathrm{innov}}{=}0$, broadcast converts
\emph{exactly} $0.0\%$ in every replicate---its entire yield is
innovator-derived---yet random still beats cluster ($41.4\pm9.3\%$ vs
$20.1\pm4.3\%$): team embedding plus the heterogeneous threshold tail
suffices. Sustained campaigning does not repair the arithmetic: a 20-step
campaign lifts broadcast only to $11.6\%$---a third of cluster, a sixth of
scattering.

\textbf{Decay reverses the ranking (Fig.~\ref{fig:decay}).} With
reinforcement-dependent relapse ($\rho{=}0.25$ while
$\mathrm{share}<\theta$), scattered gains largely evaporate---random
$69.5\%\rightarrow30.1\%$ ($-57\%$), champions $-40\%$---while cluster-seeded
adoption barely moves ($31.9\%\rightarrow31.1\%$, $-2.7\%$): everyone a pilot
team converts remains surrounded by adopters. No strategy dominates both
objectives: seeding design faces a \emph{reach--retention trade-off}. (Decay
never produces the documented ``spike-then-relapse'' shape here---relapse
interleaves with adoption; a negative result we report rather than hide.)


\textbf{Visibility: a theorem and an intervention (Fig.~\ref{fig:pilots}).}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:share}, the trajectory under global visibility
$(\{\theta_i\},v)$ is \emph{identical} to $(\{\theta_i/v\},1)$ in any
broadcast-free scenario, decay included (exact; machine-verified to bit
equality). Invisible usage is threshold inflation: it starves every strategy
uniformly (at $v\le0.6$ no strategy exceeds $8.8\%$) and provably cannot
reorder them; broadcast alone is exempt (a broadcast is loud by nature). Only
\emph{differential} visibility can matter: making seeds fully visible
(``observable pilots,'' a work-out-loud intervention) at
$v_{\mathrm{global}}{=}0.8$ yields $+4.0$\,pp for cluster seeds, $+9.5$\,pp
for random, $+15.0$\,pp for champions---proportional to outward credibility
per seed ($5.7$ / $11.7$ / $16.1$): a pilot team's megaphone mostly points at
people who already adopted. The intervention does \emph{not} rescue cluster
seeding.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The meso-structure overturns the micro-level prescription: when everyone
already lives in a dense team, guaranteed local reinforcement---cluster
seeding's defining asset---is no longer scarce, and scattering buys more
ignition surface per budget. What survives of the folklore is conditional:
cluster seeding is the \emph{retention} strategy, not the reach strategy.
Knockouts of any top-12 betweenness node (CEO included) shift the plateau by
less than replicate noise---no individual is load-bearing (the diagnostic
fires correctly on a designed bottleneck). All of this holds \emph{within a
synthetic, uncalibrated model family}: thresholds are unobserved, time is
abstract, one behavior spreads in isolation; a 19-entry limitations document
accompanies the code. Future work: imported real topologies; a stylized
historical backtest.
